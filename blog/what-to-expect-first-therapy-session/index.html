What to Expect in Your First Therapy Session (So You Can Stop Dreading It)

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC November 18, 2026 8 min read

The short answerIn a first therapy session you will review consent and confidentiality, do a quick tech check for video, and spend most of the hour answering open questions about what brought you in, how it affects your life, what you have tried, and what you want to change. You set early goals together. You do not have to share everything yet.

Key takeaways

  • Before the session you will complete consent forms, receive a Good Faith Estimate, and do a quick tech check for video.
  • The first 10 minutes cover privacy, confidentiality and one open question: what brings you here now.
  • You will be asked about history, impact, past attempts and safety, and you do not have to share trauma details until you are ready.
  • The therapeutic alliance predicts outcomes (r = .278 across 295 studies) and holds up equally well over video.
  • Most people see meaningful change within roughly 8 to 18 sessions, and the early sessions carry much of the benefit.

What happens before your first therapy session?

Once we schedule, you will receive a few forms through my secure client portal. These usually include informed consent (what therapy is, what confidentiality covers and its limits), practice policies (fees, cancellations, how to reach me between sessions), a short intake questionnaire, and a telehealth consent.

You will also receive a Good Faith Estimate. Under the federal No Surprises Act, people who are not using insurance have the right to a written estimate of expected costs before care begins.1 It is not a bill. It is a clear picture of what the work will cost so there are no surprises.

Fill the forms out honestly, but do not agonize over them. They are a starting point, not a test.

The tech check for video sessions

Modern Balance Therapy is telehealth-only, with secure video sessions available anywhere in Texas. Before we meet, find a private spot where you will not be overheard, test your camera and microphone, and use headphones if you share walls. If the connection drops, I will call you, so keep your phone nearby.

Pick a place where you can be honest. Your car in a parking lot counts. So does a closet with a pillow. Privacy matters more than a nice background.

What happens in the first 10 minutes?

I will say hello, confirm you are somewhere private, and ask where you are physically located, which is a safety requirement for telehealth. Then I will walk through confidentiality in plain language: what stays between us, and the few situations where the law requires me to act, such as imminent danger to you or someone else, or abuse of a child or vulnerable adult.

Then I will ask something like, "What brings you here now?" Not "what is wrong with you." What brings you here, and why now. There is no right answer, and you do not have to be organized about it. Most people start in the middle, and that is fine.

What questions will you be asked, and why?

A first session is mostly me listening and asking. Expect questions in a few areas, each with a reason behind it.

  • What is going on and how long it has been going on. This helps us see whether this is a recent stressor or a longer pattern.
  • How it is affecting your sleep, work, relationships and daily life. Impact tells me more than labels do.
  • What you have already tried. Past therapy, medication, books, apps, things that helped and things that did not. I do not want to repeat what has not worked for you.
  • Your history in broad strokes. Family, major life events, health. I ask because context changes the plan.
  • Safety. I will ask directly about thoughts of harming yourself. I ask everyone. It is not an accusation, and an honest answer helps me take good care of you.
  • What you want to be different. This becomes the seed of our goals.

What do you not have to share yet?

Anything you are not ready to say. This matters especially in trauma work, which is my specialty. You do not have to tell me the details of what happened to you in the first session, or the fifth. We work at your pace, and in approaches like trauma-focused CBT, building safety and coping skills comes before any processing of the memory itself.

If I ask something that feels like too much, say "I am not ready to go there." That is a complete sentence, and I will respect it. Knowing and voicing your limits is part of what makes therapy safe.

How do goals get set in therapy?

Toward the end of the first session, or early in the second, we start naming what you want from this. I usually ask, "If this works, what will be different in three months?" Then we translate that into a few concrete goals we can actually track.

Goals change. Someone who comes in for anxiety about work often discovers the anxiety has older roots. That is normal, and we adjust as we go.

What you expect from therapy matters more than people think. A meta-analysis of 81 samples with 12,722 patients found that clients' early expectations about whether treatment would help were linked to their actual outcomes (r = .18, a small but reliable effect).3 Expectations are not magic, but setting clear, realistic goals in the first session is a sensible way to build them.

What does a good fit with a therapist feel like?

The relationship between you and your therapist, what researchers call the therapeutic alliance, is one of the most consistent predictors of whether therapy helps. A meta-analysis of 295 studies with more than 30,000 patients found the alliance correlated with outcome at r = .278, and the effect was nearly identical for internet-delivered therapy (r = .275).2 In plain terms, the connection matters, and it holds up over video.

A good fit usually feels like this: you feel heard rather than analyzed. The therapist reflects back something that makes you think, "yes, that is it." You leave a little lighter, or at least less alone. You do not have to click instantly, because alliance builds over the first few sessions. But you should feel respected from the start.

What are the red flags in a first session?

  • The therapist talks more than you do, or makes the session about themselves.
  • You feel judged, rushed or lectured.
  • They promise a specific cure or a guaranteed timeline.
  • They push you to share trauma details before you are ready.
  • They are vague about fees, confidentiality or their license.
  • They brush off your questions about how they work.

Any of these is a fair reason to try someone else. Switching therapists after one or two sessions is common and not rude. The National Institute of Mental Health recommends a preliminary conversation with any therapist to see whether you feel comfortable, and notes that rapport and trust are essential.4

What happens after the first session?

We schedule the next one, usually a week out. I may give you something small to notice or try between sessions. You may feel relieved, tired, raw or all three. Talking about hard things uses energy, so plan something gentle afterward if you can.

It is also normal to replay the session and wonder if you said too much. You did not.

How long until therapy starts to help?

Faster than people fear, slower than people hope. In the foundational dose-response study of 2,431 patients, improvement climbed steeply over the first several sessions before leveling off, which means the early sessions carry a lot of the benefit.5 A later review found broad agreement that 13 to 18 sessions are needed for about half of clients to improve meaningfully, and that in well-run trials 57.6% to 67.2% of patients improved within an average of 12.7 sessions.6 Reviews of routine care put the most useful range at roughly 4 to 26 sessions depending on the problem, with weekly sessions speeding things up.7

One honest caveat: the same research found that in everyday practice many people attend fewer than five sessions and improve at much lower rates.6 Leaving early is common, with a meta-analysis of 669 studies and 83,834 clients finding that about one in five people (19.7%) stop therapy before finishing.8 The first session is where much of that is decided. If something does not feel right, say so. If it feels okay, give it a few sessions before you judge.

Common questions

Do I have to cry or talk about my childhood in the first session?

No. A first session is about understanding what brought you in and what you want to change, not about going deep. You may touch on family or history in broad strokes because context matters, but you set the depth. If you cry, that is fine. If you do not, that is fine too.

Should I prepare anything before my first therapy appointment?

Only lightly. It helps to jot down two or three things you want the therapist to know, roughly when the problem started, and what you hope will be different. Bring any medication names if relevant. Beyond that, show up as you are. The therapist's job is to guide the conversation, not grade your preparation.

What if I do not feel a connection with the therapist after the first session?

Give it two or three sessions unless something felt disrespectful or unsafe. The working relationship usually builds over the first few meetings rather than instantly. If it still feels off, say so. A good therapist will talk it through and, if needed, help you find a better fit without taking it personally.
