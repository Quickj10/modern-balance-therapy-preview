When Should Couples Go to Therapy? Signs It Is Time, Before It Is a Crisis

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC April 14, 2026 8 min read

The short answerCouples benefit most from therapy when they come early: when the same fight keeps repeating, when one partner pursues and the other shuts down, when intimacy drifts, or when a major transition or trust injury is straining the bond. Research shows most couples wait until problems are severe, and earlier help works better.

Key takeaways

  • Peer-reviewed research shows most couples delay therapy until problems are severe, and only about a third of newlyweds seek any help in their first five years.
  • Signs it is time include repeating fights, pursue-and-withdraw loops, intimacy drift, big transitions, trust injuries, parenting conflict and engagement.
  • Couples therapy is not refereeing; the relationship is the client and both partners work on the pattern.
  • Emotionally focused and behavioral couples therapy both show medium to large effects, with gains that can hold for years when couples do not wait too long.
  • Active abuse or untreated addiction means individual therapy should come first, and if your partner will not come, starting alone still changes the pattern.

How long do couples wait before getting help?

You may have seen the claim that couples wait an average of six years after problems begin before they seek help. It is quoted everywhere, and it may well be true, but it traces back to an unpublished 1992 study, so I am not going to lean on it.

What the peer-reviewed research shows is just as telling. Researchers at UCLA surveyed 147 couples who were in the process of seeking marital therapy and opened their paper with the problem in one line: despite proven effectiveness, couples generally do not seek help until their problems become severe.1 They found that getting to therapy involves three separate steps, recognizing the problem, considering treatment and actually seeking it, and that on average wives completed all three before their husbands.1

A later study followed 213 couples through their first five years of marriage. Only 36 percent sought any outside help during that time, and that counted everything from reading a relationship book to seeing a therapist.2 Difficulties in one year predicted help-seeking in the next, which means most couples wait until things are already hurting.2

I share this not to scold anyone. I share it because the single most common thing I hear from couples in a first session is, "We should have come sooner."

What are the signs it is time for couples therapy?

You do not need to be on the edge of separating. These are the patterns that tell me a couple would benefit from coming in now rather than later.

  • The same fight, on repeat. The topic changes (dishes, money, in-laws) but the shape of the argument never does, and it never resolves.
  • Communication loops. One of you pursues, the other shuts down, and the pursuing gets louder as the shutting down gets quieter.
  • Intimacy drift. Less touch, less sex, less talking about anything other than logistics. Roommates with a shared calendar.
  • A big transition. A new baby, a move, a job loss, a diagnosis, retirement, blending families. Good changes strain a relationship too.
  • A trust injury. An affair, a financial secret, a broken promise that keeps resurfacing even after the apology.
  • Parenting disagreements that have turned into a tug of war where the kids can feel the rope.
  • You are engaged or planning to be. Pre-marital counseling is not a sign something is wrong. It is a head start.
  • One of you is quietly keeping score, or quietly wondering whether to stay.

If two or three of these feel familiar, that is reason enough to book a consult. You are not failing by asking for help. You are early, which is the best time to be.

What is couples therapy, and what is it not?

Couples therapy is structured help for the relationship itself. The relationship is the client. My job is to slow the conversation down enough that each of you can hear what the other is actually saying underneath the complaint, and then help you build new patterns on purpose.

Here is what it is not. It is not a courtroom, and I am not a referee. I do not decide who is right. I do not take sides, and I will not secretly agree with one of you after the other leaves. If you come hoping I will fix your partner, I will gently redirect that, because it never works and because both of you are usually carrying part of the pattern.

It is also not a last resort. Couples therapy works best when there is still goodwill to build on.

Does couples therapy actually work?

Yes, and the evidence is stronger than most people expect.

A review of 20 meta-analyses of marital and family interventions concluded that therapy for distressed couples is effective, that the gains shrink only slightly at follow-up, and that roughly 40 to 50 percent of those treated show clinically significant improvement.4 A decade-long review in the Journal of Marital and Family Therapy put it more simply: couple therapy positively impacts about 70 percent of couples who receive it, with effectiveness comparable to individual therapy and far better than no treatment.3

Two approaches have the deepest research base, and I draw on both.

Emotionally focused therapy (EFT) works on the attachment bond underneath the fighting. It helps each partner see the vulnerable feeling (fear, loneliness, feeling not enough) that is driving the protest or the withdrawal. Behavioral couples therapy focuses on concrete skills: how you raise a complaint, how you respond to a bid for attention, how you negotiate the practical stuff.

A meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials with 2,730 participants found medium effects on relationship satisfaction right after treatment for both approaches (overall g = 0.60; EFT g = 0.73; behavioral g = 0.53), with gains still present at six months.5 The two did not differ significantly from each other. The authors also note that in the behavioral trials that measured 12 months out, the gains had faded, which is a fair reminder that maintenance matters.5

The longest follow-up I know of comes from a trial of 134 seriously and chronically distressed married couples. Five years after about eight months of behavioral couple therapy, roughly half showed clinically significant improvement in marital satisfaction, with large effect sizes relative to where they started (d = 0.92 to 1.03).6 About a quarter had separated or divorced, which the authors note compares favorably with other long-term couple therapy results given how distressed these couples were going in.6 I read that study two ways: therapy helps even late, and it helps more when you do not wait.

Pre-marital work has its own evidence. A meta-analysis of premarital prevention programs found a mean effect size of 0.80, meaning the average participant did better afterward than 79 percent of couples who did not take part, with the clearest gains in communication skills and relationship quality.7

What happens in the first couples session?

The first session is a conversation, not a test. I ask each of you how you see the relationship right now, what brought you in and what you hope will be different. I will ask about your history as a couple: how you met, what was good, when things shifted.

I am listening for the pattern, not the villain. Most couples have one core cycle that shows up in every fight, and naming it together is often the first relief anyone has felt in months.

We also agree on ground rules. Both of you get air time. Nobody gets ambushed. And I will tell you honestly whether couples therapy is the right next step, or whether something else should come first.

Video works surprisingly well for couples: you are on your own couch, in your own space, and nobody has to coordinate a drive across town.

When is individual therapy the better first step?

Sometimes couples work is not the safest or most useful place to start, and I will say so.

Active abuse. If there is ongoing physical violence, intimidation or coercive control, couples therapy can make things more dangerous by asking the person being harmed to speak openly in front of the person harming them. Individual support, and a safety plan, come first.

Untreated addiction. When one partner's drinking or drug use is active and unaddressed, the relationship work keeps collapsing back into it. Treating the addiction first gives the couples work a chance.

Unresolved trauma or a severe mood episode. Trauma and PTSD are my specialty, and sometimes what looks like a relationship problem is one partner's nervous system reacting to old injuries. A stretch of individual trauma work can change the couples conversation entirely.

None of this means your relationship is beyond help. It means the order matters.

How long does couples therapy take?

Shorter than most people fear. Many couples see a meaningful shift in 8 to 12 sessions, especially when the problem is a specific cycle or a specific transition. Trust injuries and long-standing patterns take longer, often several months of weekly work followed by a taper. The five-year trial above used roughly eight months, and that was for seriously, chronically distressed couples.6

Pre-marital counseling is usually the shortest: a handful of sessions to talk through money, family, faith, conflict style, sex and the expectations nobody has said out loud yet.

I also use solution-focused brief therapy when a couple wants something targeted and time-limited. We define the goal, build on what already works, and stop when you get there.

What if my partner will not come?

This is more common than you think, and it is not a dead end.

First, ask differently. "We need therapy" lands as an accusation. "I want us to be closer and I do not know how to get there on my own" is an invitation. Offer a single consult with no commitment beyond that.

Second, come on your own. Individual therapy focused on your side of the pattern changes the pattern, because a cycle needs two people to run it. Many reluctant partners join later once they see the conversation at home has changed.

Third, give it time without giving up. Reluctance is often fear of being blamed. Once your partner hears that a referee is not what they are walking into, the door tends to open.

Common questions

Do we have to be married to come to couples therapy?

No. I work with married couples, engaged couples doing pre-marital counseling, and committed partners who are dating or living together. What matters is that both of you want the relationship to be better and are willing to look at your own part of the pattern. Couples sessions are for adults; teens 12 and up are seen individually.

Can we do couples therapy online from different locations?

Yes. Most couples join from the same room on one screen, but if work or travel keeps you apart you can each join from your own device. Both of you need to be physically in Texas for the session, since my license covers Texas. A private, quiet space on each end makes the biggest difference to how useful the hour is.

Does insurance cover couples therapy?

I do not accept insurance, so couples sessions are paid directly at $200 for 60 to 80 minutes. Many health plans do not reimburse relationship counseling at all, so I would check your out-of-network benefits before counting on it. If your plan does reimburse, ask about a superbill at your consult and I will walk you through what it includes.
