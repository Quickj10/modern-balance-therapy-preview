How Do You Know If You Need Therapy? 12 Signs It Might Be Time

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC November 18, 2026 7 min read

The short answerYou might need therapy if a low mood, worry or numbness has lasted more than a few weeks, if sleep, work or relationships are suffering, if your coping has shifted to things that cost you later, or if people close to you have noticed a change. You do not need a diagnosis or a crisis to start.

Key takeaways

  • Functioning is not the same as okay; therapy is for people who are tired of what it costs them to keep going.
  • Twelve common signs fall into four groups: how you feel, how you function, how you cope, and what others notice.
  • Therapy works, with large effects for depression (g = 0.79 across 409 trials) and moderate effects for anxiety and PTSD.
  • You do not need a diagnosis or a crisis; early help lowers the risk of a problem becoming a bigger one.
  • A free 15-minute consult is a low-pressure way to find out whether therapy is a fit.

Why is it so hard to know if you need therapy?

Most people do not wake up one day certain they need a therapist. The question usually arrives sideways: you are snappier than you want to be, sleep is off, and you keep telling yourself it is just a busy season.

You are in good company. In 2022, about 23.1% of U.S. adults, roughly 59.3 million people, lived with a mental illness, and only about half of them received any treatment that year.1 Among adults who felt they needed help and did not get it, 70.5% said they thought they should have been able to handle their mental health on their own.2

That "I should be able to handle this" belief is the one I hear most often in consults. So let me offer a clearer way to look at the question.

Functioning is not the same as okay

Many of the people I work with are holding down jobs, raising kids and showing up for everyone around them. From the outside they look fine. On the inside, it takes everything they have.

Therapy is not reserved for people who have stopped functioning. It is for people who are tired of how much it costs them to keep functioning. If you have been grading yourself on whether you can still get through the day, try a different question: what is getting through the day costing you?

12 signs it might be time for therapy

None of these signs is a diagnosis, and no single one means something is wrong with you. They are patterns worth noticing. If several feel familiar, or one has stuck around for more than a few weeks, that is a reasonable signal to talk with someone.

How you feel

  1. The low mood or worry has outlasted its reason. A rough month after a loss, a breakup or a move makes sense. Feeling flat, on edge or hopeless for weeks after things have settled is different.
  2. You feel numb or disconnected. Things you used to enjoy feel like chores, or you feel like you are watching your life rather than living it.
  3. Your emotions feel too big for the moment. Small frustrations turn into anger or tears you cannot explain, and you are left wondering where that came from.

How you function

  1. Sleep, appetite or energy has changed and stayed changed. Trouble falling asleep, waking at 3 a.m. with your mind racing, or eating much more or much less than usual.
  2. Work or school is harder than it should be. Concentration slips, tasks pile up, and you spend more energy covering than doing.
  3. Your relationships are taking a hit. You are pulling away, picking fights, or finding it hard to be present with the people you love.

How you cope

  1. Your coping has shifted to things that cost you later. More drinks than you planned, scrolling until 2 a.m., overworking, or eating to quiet a feeling.
  2. You keep circling the same problem without moving. You have thought about it, journaled about it and talked to friends, and you still end up in the same place.
  3. Something from the past keeps showing up in the present. A memory, a reaction or a sense of being on guard that does not match what is actually happening. In my practice this is often the first clue that an old experience is still asking for attention.

What others notice

  1. Someone who loves you has said something. "You seem different lately" is rarely said casually.
  2. You have started hiding how you are doing. You give the short answer because the real answer feels like too much to put on anyone.
  3. You have thought about therapy more than once. This one counts. If the idea keeps coming back, part of you already suspects it would help.

Does therapy actually work?

Yes, and that is not just a therapist's opinion. In 2012 the American Psychological Association formally adopted a resolution recognizing psychotherapy as an effective treatment, based on decades of outcome research.3

The research is specific. A 2023 meta-analysis of 409 trials with 52,702 patients found cognitive behavioral therapy outperformed usual care or waiting lists for depression with an effect size of g = 0.79, a large effect that was still present at 6 to 12 month follow-up.4 For anxiety and related conditions, a meta-analysis of 41 placebo-controlled trials with 2,835 patients found a moderate overall effect (g = 0.56) for CBT, including a meaningful effect for PTSD.5

An effect size is a way of measuring how much a treatment moves the needle compared with doing nothing. A number around 0.5 is considered moderate and around 0.8 is considered large. These are not small differences.

What therapy is NOT for

You do not need a diagnosis to start. A meta-analysis of 18 trials with 1,913 participants found that therapy helped people whose depressive symptoms fell below the threshold for a diagnosis, and it lowered their risk of developing major depression over the next six months by about 39%.6 Getting help early is not overreacting. It is the version of this story with the best odds.

You also do not need to be in crisis. Most of my clients come in because life has gotten heavier than it needs to be, not because something has broken. And you do not need to know what is wrong or have the words for it yet. Finding the words is part of the work.

Therapy is also not a place where someone tells you who you are. A good therapist helps you see your own patterns more clearly and decide what you want to do about them. The approach should fit you, too: I draw on CBT, trauma-focused CBT, psychodynamic work, solution-focused brief therapy, person-centered and family systems approaches depending on what you need, not the other way around.

How long does it take to feel a difference?

Sooner than most people expect. In the foundational dose-response study of 2,431 patients, improvement rose steeply over the first several sessions and then leveled off, with most of the gain happening early.7 A later review of that literature found general agreement that 13 to 18 sessions are needed for about half of clients to improve in a meaningful way, and that in well-run trials 57.6% to 67.2% of patients improved within an average of 12.7 sessions.8

In my practice, people often notice something shift within the first handful of sessions: a little more room to breathe, a pattern they can finally see. Deeper work, especially trauma work, takes longer, and we pace it together.

Common questions

Can I go to therapy if nothing is really wrong?

Yes. Many people start therapy for a life transition, a recurring relationship pattern, or a sense of being stuck rather than a specific problem. Research shows therapy helps even when symptoms fall below the level of a diagnosis, and early support lowers the chance that a hard season turns into something heavier.

How do I know if I need therapy or just a break?

A break helps when rest actually restores you. If you take time off and the low mood, worry or irritability returns within days, or if you cannot really rest because your mind will not slow down, that is a sign the issue is not fatigue alone. Therapy helps you address what rest cannot.

Is online therapy a real option for a first time in therapy?

It is. Secure video sessions let you talk from a private space you choose, which many first-time clients find easier than an office. Research on the therapeutic relationship finds it is just as strong online as in person, and telehealth removes commute time and scheduling friction that keep people from starting.
