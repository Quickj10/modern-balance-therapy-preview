Does Online Therapy Work? What the Research Says About Telehealth Counseling

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC November 18, 2026 8 min read

The short answerYes. For depression, anxiety and PTSD, video therapy produces outcomes that are statistically no different from in-person therapy in head-to-head trials and meta-analyses, and the therapist relationship still forms over a screen. Online therapy is not the right level of care for an acute crisis or for very young children, but for most adults and teens it works.

Key takeaways

  • Meta-analyses covering thousands of clients find video therapy and in-person therapy produce essentially the same outcomes, with the strongest results for CBT and for anxiety, depression and PTSD.
  • Randomized VA trials of Prolonged Exposure and Cognitive Processing Therapy found home-based video delivery noninferior to in-person treatment for PTSD, and the 2023 VA/DoD guideline strongly recommends secure video delivery.
  • The therapeutic relationship forms over video; a 2018 review found symptom improvement noninferior even when alliance ratings were slightly lower on paper.
  • Online therapy is not the right fit for an acute crisis, psychosis, severe withdrawal or very young children, who need in-person or emergency care first.
  • A private room, headphones, a stable camera and a short buffer before and after the session make a measurable difference in how a video session feels.

What is online therapy, exactly?

Online therapy, also called telehealth counseling or teletherapy, is regular talk therapy delivered over a secure, live video connection instead of in an office. You and your therapist see and hear each other in real time. The session length, the methods and the licensing requirements are the same as they would be in person.

It helps to separate live video therapy from two things that often get lumped in with it. Text-only messaging apps and self-guided online programs are different products with a different evidence base. When I say online therapy in this article, I mean a scheduled video session with a licensed therapist.

Modern Balance Therapy is fully virtual. Every session I offer is held by secure video with clients anywhere in Texas, ages 12 and up.

Does online therapy work as well as in-person therapy?

This is the question I get asked most, and the research is now large enough to answer it with some confidence. A 2021 meta-analysis in Clinical Psychology and Psychotherapy pooled 56 within-group studies with 1,681 participants and 47 between-group studies with 3,564 participants. Video therapy beat wait-list controls with an effect size of g = 0.77, and the difference between video and in-person therapy was negligible.1

The same review found the strongest effects for cognitive behavioral therapy (g = 1.34, versus 0.66 for non-CBT approaches) and for anxiety, depression and PTSD specifically.1 Those happen to be the concerns most people bring to therapy, and they are the areas I work in most.

A second meta-analysis, published in Clinical Psychology Review the same year, looked at 57 studies covering 281 treatment outcomes across 4,336 clients. The authors concluded that videoconferencing produced effects "largely equivalent" to in-person care.2

Internet-delivered CBT with therapist support has also been reviewed by Cochrane, the most cautious reviewer in medicine. Across 38 randomized trials and 3,214 adults with anxiety disorders, therapist-supported online CBT outperformed wait lists (standardized mean difference of -1.06) and showed no meaningful difference from face-to-face CBT in the seven trials that compared them directly.3

What about trauma and PTSD specifically?

Some of the strongest head-to-head evidence comes from the Department of Veterans Affairs, which had to answer this question for rural veterans long before 2020. In one randomized noninferiority trial, 132 veterans with PTSD received Prolonged Exposure therapy either in person or by video into their own homes. The home-based video group did just as well at the end of treatment and at three and six month follow-up.4

A separate trial randomized 125 rural veterans to Cognitive Processing Therapy by video or in person. Video delivery was noninferior, both groups improved substantially (Cohen's d = 0.78), and ratings of therapeutic alliance, attendance and satisfaction were high with no difference between formats.5

The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline for PTSD now carries a strong recommendation for delivering its first-line therapies by secure video teleconferencing when the therapy has been validated in that format or when other options are unavailable.6 That matters to me as a trauma therapist. It means the treatments I rely on were tested over video, not just assumed to transfer.

Can you really build a relationship with a therapist through a screen?

The therapeutic relationship, which researchers call the working alliance, is one of the most reliable predictors of whether therapy helps. So it is a fair worry. A 2018 systematic review of 12 studies found that every one of them reported good alliance and good outcomes for video therapy.7

When the authors pooled the six studies with face-to-face comparison groups, alliance ratings were slightly lower over video (standardized mean difference of -0.30), though the difference was not statistically significant. Symptom improvement, the outcome that actually matters, was noninferior (-0.03).7 In plain terms, people may rate the connection a touch lower on paper, and they get better just the same.

In my own experience, the relationship forms the same way it does in an office. It is built on feeling heard, on consistency and on trust, and none of those require sharing a room. Many clients tell me they open up faster from their own couch than they would in a waiting room.

Who is online therapy a good fit for, and who should look elsewhere?

Online therapy suits most adults and teens dealing with anxiety, depression, trauma, grief, relationship strain or a hard life transition. It is especially helpful if you travel for work, care for small children, manage a chronic illness or live far from a specialist.

It is not the right level of care for everyone. If you are in an acute crisis, actively planning to harm yourself or someone else, or experiencing psychosis or severe substance withdrawal, you need in-person or emergency services first. Some conditions also need a care team that includes a prescriber, or more structure than weekly video sessions can provide.

Very young children generally do better with in-person, play-based work, which is one reason my practice starts at age 12. If I think you need something I cannot offer over video, I will tell you plainly and help you find it.

Is online therapy private?

It should be, and it is reasonable to ask your therapist how. Under the HIPAA Security Rule, providers must protect the confidentiality, integrity and availability of electronic health information and guard against reasonably anticipated threats to it.8 In practice, that means a therapist should use a video platform built for health care, with encryption and a signed business associate agreement, rather than a consumer app.

I use a HIPAA-compliant video platform for every session, and I hold sessions from a private space. Your end of the connection is the part only you can control. A closed door, headphones and a quick check of who else is on your network go a long way.

How do you get the most out of a therapy session at home?

A few practical habits make a real difference, and none of them are complicated.

  • Pick one spot and keep it. A bedroom, a parked car or a quiet corner works. Familiar surroundings help your mind shift into session mode.
  • Use headphones. They keep your side private and make it easier to hear tone of voice, which carries a lot in therapy.
  • Prop up your device. A stable camera at eye level feels more like a conversation than holding a phone.
  • Test the connection five minutes early. Close other apps and, if you can, sit near the router.
  • Build a short buffer on both ends. Walking straight from a work call into therapy and back is hard on anyone. Give yourself a moment to arrive and a moment to land afterward.
  • Tell your household. A note on the door or a shared calendar block prevents the mid-session interruption.

Why telehealth matters in a state the size of Texas

Texas has 254 counties, and many rural ones have few mental health providers, let alone someone who specializes in trauma. Video sessions remove the drive. A client two hundred miles from the nearest trauma therapist can see one on a Tuesday afternoon without leaving town.

Distance is not the only barrier. Some people live in a small community where walking into a counseling office means being seen. Others have a job or a family schedule that makes a midday appointment plus travel time impossible, and online therapy quietly solves both.

Because I hold a Texas LPC license, I can see clients anywhere in the state. Modern Balance Therapy is secure telehealth only, which keeps my overhead low and lets me offer straightforward session fees without insurance paperwork.

A note from my practice

When I moved to fully virtual work, I expected to miss something. What I found instead was that the work itself did not change: clients still cry, still laugh, still have the session where something finally shifts. The only thing missing is the commute.

Common questions

Does insurance cover online therapy?

Many insurers now treat video sessions like office visits, but it depends on your plan. Modern Balance Therapy does not bill insurance directly. Sessions are $150 to $175 for individuals and $200 for couples. If you want to seek out-of-network reimbursement, ask about a superbill at your consult, and if cost is a barrier, ask about sliding scale availability at the same time.

Can teenagers do online therapy?

Yes. I see clients ages 12 and up, and teens often take to video sessions quickly because the format is familiar to them. A parent or guardian provides consent and is part of the first conversation, and then the teen needs a private space, headphones and the understanding that the session is their time. Family check-ins are built in where they help.

What happens if my internet drops in the middle of a session?

We plan for it before it happens. At your first session we agree on a backup, usually a phone call to the number you give me, so a dropped connection costs a minute rather than the session. If technology fails on my end, the time is made up. Sitting close to your router and closing other apps prevents most interruptions.
