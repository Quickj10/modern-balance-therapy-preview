Stress or Anxiety? How to Tell the Difference and When to Get Help

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC June 9, 2026 8 min read

The short answerStress is your response to a specific demand and usually eases when the demand does. Anxiety is apprehension about a vague or future threat that can linger after the stressor is gone, or arrive without one. Worry that is hard to control most days for six months and interferes with sleep, work or relationships is a signal to get help.

Key takeaways

  • Stress is a reaction to an external demand; anxiety is future-oriented apprehension that can persist without a clear trigger.
  • The same fight-or-flight chemistry drives both, and chronic stress that never gets to stand down is a common on-ramp to an anxiety disorder.
  • Generalized anxiety is marked by worry that is hard to control on most days for at least six months, plus physical symptoms like restlessness, fatigue, muscle tension and poor sleep.
  • About 19 percent of U.S. adults have an anxiety disorder in a given year, and 22 percent rated their stress at 8 or higher out of 10 in the 2024 Stress in America survey.
  • CBT shows a large effect for generalized anxiety (g = 0.84), and exercise and improved sleep each produce moderate, measurable reductions in anxiety.

What is the difference between stress and anxiety?

The two words get used interchangeably, and in everyday life the feelings overlap. But they are not the same thing, and knowing which one you are dealing with changes what will help.

The American Psychological Association defines stress as "the physiological or psychological response to internal or external stressors."1 In plain words, stress is your reaction to a demand. There is a deadline, a sick parent, a bill, a toddler who will not sleep, and your body and mind gear up to deal with it. Stress has a cause you can usually point to, and it tends to ease when the demand does.

Anxiety, by contrast, is "an emotion characterized by apprehension and somatic symptoms of tension," in which you anticipate danger or misfortune that may be vague or far off.2 The APA separates it from fear this way: fear is a short-lived response to a specific threat in front of you, while anxiety is future-oriented and long-lasting, aimed at a diffuse threat. Anxiety can persist after the stressor is gone, and it can show up when there is no stressor at all.

Stress vs anxiety: a side-by-side

  • Trigger. Stress: a specific external demand. Anxiety: often internal, vague or anticipated, and sometimes there is no clear trigger.
  • Timing. Stress: rises with the demand and falls when it resolves. Anxiety: lingers, loops, or shows up well ahead of time.
  • Focus. Stress: "I have too much to handle." Anxiety: "Something bad is going to happen."
  • Body. Both: tense muscles, racing heart, poor sleep, upset stomach. Anxiety adds persistent restlessness and dread, even at rest.
  • Response to rest. Stress: a weekend off or finishing the project usually helps. Anxiety: rest often does not touch it, and sometimes just makes room for more worry.
  • Useful side. Stress: in doses, it sharpens focus and gets things done. Anxiety: occasional anxiety is normal and protective, while persistent anxiety mostly gets in the way.

One more wrinkle: chronic stress is one of the most common on-ramps to anxiety. When the demands never let up, the body stops standing down between them, and the alarm system starts firing on its own schedule.

What happens in your body when you are stressed?

When your brain registers a threat, real or imagined, it signals the adrenal glands to release adrenaline and then cortisol. Adrenaline speeds your heart, quickens your breathing, tightens your muscles and sharpens your senses, while cortisol keeps the system primed, raises blood sugar for quick energy, and temporarily turns down functions that can wait, like digestion and immune activity. This is the fight-or-flight response, and it is a brilliant piece of design when the threat is a car swerving into your lane.

The trouble starts when the alarm stays on, because the system was built for short bursts followed by recovery, not for a job, a marriage or a news cycle that never quite resolves. Living with the stress response switched on for weeks or months shows up as fatigue, poor sleep, headaches, muscle pain, digestive trouble, frequent illness and a short fuse. It also keeps the brain's threat detector sensitized, so smaller and smaller things set it off: that is the bridge from stress to anxiety, and it is physical, not a character flaw.

When does worry cross into an anxiety disorder?

Everyone worries. The question is not whether you worry but how much, for how long, and at what cost. For generalized anxiety disorder, the most common pattern of chronic worry, the National Institute of Mental Health describes worry that is hard to control, present on most days for at least six months, along with at least three of the following: restlessness or feeling on edge, being easily tired, trouble concentrating, irritability, muscle tension and sleep problems.3

In plain words, three things tend to mark the line.

  • Duration: it has been going on for months, not a rough week.
  • Control: you cannot talk yourself out of it even when you know it is out of proportion.
  • Interference: it is costing you sleep, focus, relationships, work or things you used to enjoy.

I am describing a pattern, not diagnosing you. Only an evaluation with a licensed professional can do that. But if you read those three markers and nodded at all of them, that is worth taking seriously.

How common are stress and anxiety?

Very. In the APA's 2024 Stress in America survey of 3,305 U.S. adults, the average stress rating was 5.0 on a 10-point scale, and 22 percent of adults rated their stress over the past month at 8 or higher. Health (66 percent), work (65 percent of employed adults) and money (64 percent) were the most common significant stressors, and 77 percent named the future of the nation.4

Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health condition in the country. According to NIMH, an estimated 19.1 percent of U.S. adults had an anxiety disorder in the past year and 31.1 percent experience one in their lifetime. Women are affected more often than men (23.4 percent versus 14.3 percent in the past year), and nearly a third of adolescents aged 13 to 18 meet criteria at some point.5

If you are struggling with this, you have a great deal of company. You also have a condition with some of the most effective treatments in mental health.

What actually helps, according to the research?

Therapy, especially CBT. Cognitive behavioral therapy, which NIMH describes as the gold-standard psychotherapy for generalized anxiety,3 teaches you to notice the thoughts that drive the worry, test them against reality, and change the avoidance that keeps anxiety alive. A meta-analysis of 41 studies with 2,132 patients with generalized anxiety disorder found a large effect for psychotherapy versus control conditions (g = 0.84), with a large effect on depression symptoms as well (g = 0.71).6

In my practice I combine CBT with solution-focused and person-centered work, because the skills land better when they are built around what matters to you rather than a worksheet.

Exercise. Movement is not a substitute for treatment, but it is a real intervention. A meta-analysis of six randomized trials in people diagnosed with anxiety or stress-related disorders found that exercise reduced anxiety symptoms more than control conditions, with a moderate effect size (standardized mean difference of -0.58).7 Brisk walking counts, and so does anything that raises your heart rate a few times a week.

Sleep. Poor sleep and anxiety feed each other, and improving sleep turns out to be one of the most direct levers. A 2021 meta-analysis of 65 randomized trials with 8,608 participants found that interventions that improved sleep produced medium-sized improvements in anxiety (g = -0.51) and stress (g = -0.42), and the more sleep improved, the more mental health did.8

Reducing the load where you can. For stress specifically, sometimes the honest answer is that the demand is too high, and part of the work is deciding what to set down. Therapy can help with that too, especially when guilt is the thing keeping everything on the list.

When should you get help for stress or anxiety?

You do not need to wait until you cannot function. These are the signals I would take seriously:

  • The worry or stress has lasted more than a few weeks and is not easing with rest or problem-solving.
  • Sleep, appetite or concentration are suffering.
  • You are avoiding things, people or places you used to handle.
  • You are using alcohol, food, scrolling or work to turn the volume down.
  • People who love you have said something.
  • You have physical symptoms your doctor cannot explain.
  • You have had thoughts that life is not worth the effort. This one means reaching out today, not next month.

Common questions

Can stress cause chest tightness and other physical symptoms?

Yes. The stress response tightens muscles, including the ones across your chest and shoulders, and speeds breathing and heart rate, which can feel alarming. Headaches, jaw pain, stomach upset and a racing heart are all common. Because chest symptoms can also have medical causes, new or severe chest pain should be checked by a physician first. Once that is ruled out, therapy can address the stress driving it.

Is it normal to feel anxious all the time?

Occasional anxiety is normal and even useful. Feeling anxious most of the time is common, but it is not something you have to accept as your baseline. When anxiety is present on most days, is hard to switch off, and is affecting sleep, focus or relationships, it is worth an evaluation. That pattern responds well to treatment, and most people notice a difference within the first several sessions.

Do I need medication for anxiety?

Not necessarily. Psychotherapy, particularly CBT, is an effective first-line treatment for most anxiety disorders, and many people do well with therapy alone. Some people benefit from medication as well, especially when symptoms are severe or sleep is badly disrupted. As a counselor I do not prescribe, but I can coordinate with your physician or psychiatrist if medication is something you want to explore.
