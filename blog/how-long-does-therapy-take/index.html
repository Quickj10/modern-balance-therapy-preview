How Long Does Therapy Take? What to Expect From Short-Term and Longer Work

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC November 18, 2026 8 min read

The short answerThere is no fixed number. Research suggests about half of clients improve meaningfully somewhere between 8 and 20 sessions, depending on the study and how improvement is measured. A focused problem often takes 6 to 16 sessions, trauma protocols 8 to 16, and long-standing patterns months to a year or more.

Key takeaways

  • Improvement follows a curve: most change happens in the early sessions, and estimates for 50% of clients improving range from 8 to about 18 sessions depending on the study.
  • A single focused goal with SFBT or CBT typically takes 6 to 16 sessions; structured trauma protocols run roughly 8 to 16; psychodynamic or long-term work runs months to a year or more.
  • Between-session practice, weekly consistency, honest feedback and a strong working alliance are the factors most linked to faster, more durable results.
  • Good therapy ends on purpose, with goals checked along the way, a planned taper and booster sessions available.
  • A free consult is the fastest way to turn these ranges into a realistic estimate and budget for your own situation.

The honest answer: it depends on your goals, not a formula

If you have been putting off therapy because you are not sure what you are signing up for, this is the article I wish every new client had read first.

There is no fixed number of sessions. How long therapy takes depends on what you want to change, how long the pattern has been there, how much life is happening around you, and how the two of us work together. Anyone who promises you a number before hearing your story is guessing.

What I can give you is what the research says about typical timelines, what tends to shorten or lengthen them, and how we decide together when you are done. Then a free consult can turn those ranges into an estimate for your situation.

What does the research say about how many sessions it takes?

Researchers call this the dose-response relationship, and it has been studied for forty years.

The landmark study pooled 2,431 patients from 15 studies and found that improvement follows a curve, not a straight line: most of the change happens in the early sessions, usually by session 8, with smaller gains after that.14 From that work came the often-quoted estimate that about 25% of clients improve after a single session and about 50% by session 8.2

Later studies using stricter definitions found slower timelines. When Lambert's group tracked 45 outpatients session by session and asked not "did they feel better" but "did they recover to a healthy range," only 22% had recovered by session 8.2 A review of the clinical trials literature concluded that 13 to 18 sessions are needed for 50% of clients to improve, and that in carefully delivered treatments 57.6% to 67.2% of clients improve within an average of 12.7 sessions.3

A 2019 systematic review of 26 studies of real-world therapy found the same curved pattern and put the optimal dose in routine settings between 4 and 26 sessions depending on the setting, the problem and how you measure improvement. It also found that weekly sessions speed up the rate of improvement compared with less frequent schedules.4

So the honest summary from the science: roughly half of clients improve meaningfully somewhere between 8 and 20 sessions, with the exact point depending on the study and how "improved" is defined. Most people feel some relief early, and the deeper, more durable changes take longer.

How long does therapy take for a specific problem?

When you come in with one clear target, such as a panic pattern, a work conflict you keep replaying, trouble sleeping, or a decision you cannot make, short-term work fits well.

Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) are built for this. In my practice, a focused goal usually takes 6 to 16 weekly sessions.

SFBT tends to sit at the shorter end because it concentrates on what is already working and what the next small step is. CBT for anxiety or low mood tends to run 12 to 16 because it includes learning skills, practicing them between sessions, and troubleshooting what gets in the way.

One honest caveat: "a specific problem" sometimes turns out to be the visible part of something older. If that happens, we name it, and you decide whether to extend the work or finish the original goal first.

How long does trauma therapy take?

Trauma and PTSD are my specialty, so this is the question I get most often, and the answer is more encouraging than most people expect.

The treatments with the strongest evidence are structured. A systematic review of 114 randomized trials with 8,171 adults found robust evidence for trauma-focused CBT and EMDR, and named Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT), Cognitive Therapy and Prolonged Exposure (PE) as the approaches with the strongest support.8 These protocols, along with Trauma-Focused CBT (TF-CBT) for teens, are designed to run in roughly 8 to 16 sessions, usually weekly.

That number assumes a single trauma, reasonable stability in your life, and that you are ready to approach the memory rather than avoid it. Complex trauma, meaning repeated or early experiences, often needs a longer runway: several sessions building safety and coping skills before the trauma-focused phase begins, and sometimes more than one round. Timelines of six months to a year are common and are not a sign that anything is going wrong.

How long does longer-term or psychodynamic therapy take?

Some goals are not a single problem. "I keep ending up in the same kind of relationship." "I have felt this way since childhood." "I function fine and I am not okay." Those patterns live in how you relate to yourself and others, and changing them takes time.

Psychodynamic therapy works at that level: looking at where patterns came from, how they show up in the room, and what you want instead. The research reflects the timeline. A meta-analysis in JAMA that defined long-term psychodynamic therapy as at least a year or 50 sessions pooled 23 studies with 1,053 patients and found large, stable effects for people with complex or long-standing conditions, with those patients ending up better off than most of the people in shorter comparison treatments.7 That analysis drew criticism for its statistical methods, so I hold the exact size of the effect loosely, but the direction is consistent with what I see.

In practice, longer-term work means months to a year or more, often weekly at first and then every other week as things settle. It is less about a finish line and more about building something that lasts.

What makes therapy go faster?

Four things, and most of them are in your hands.

  • Between-session practice. A meta-analysis of 17 CBT studies with 2,312 clients found that how much homework clients completed was strongly linked to better outcomes at the end of treatment (effect size 0.79) and still at follow-up (0.51).6 Therapy is one hour; the week is 167 more.
  • Consistency. Weekly sessions early on build momentum. The research on routine care is sobering here: in a database of more than 6,000 clients, the average number of sessions attended was under five, and only about 20% improved.3 Showing up is most of the battle.
  • Honest feedback. If something I said landed wrong, or a technique feels pointless, tell me. Adjusting course in week three beats drifting for three months.
  • A good alliance. The working relationship between you and your therapist is one of the most reliable predictors of outcome. A synthesis of 295 studies with more than 30,000 clients found a consistent link between alliance and results (r = .278, about a medium effect), and the link was almost identical for therapy delivered online (r = .275).5 That last finding is why I am comfortable offering secure video sessions to clients across Texas.

What slows therapy down?

None of these are character flaws. They are just factors worth naming so we can plan around them.

  • Long gaps. Monthly sessions can work for maintenance, but early on they mean re-covering old ground each time.
  • Ongoing crisis. If every session is spent on this week's fire, the deeper work waits. Sometimes stabilizing first is the right plan.
  • Avoidance. Especially in trauma work, skipping the hard sessions is understandable and also the main thing that stretches the timeline.
  • Goals that keep moving. If what we are working on changes every month, we never finish anything. We can hold more than one goal, but we name the order.
  • Untreated sleep, substance use or medical issues. These make everything slower, and a conversation with your doctor can be part of the plan.

How do you and your therapist decide when to stop?

Good therapy ends on purpose. From the first session, we set goals in your words and check progress against them, often with a short questionnaire every few weeks so the trend is visible rather than guessed.

When the goals are met, we plan the ending rather than just drifting apart. That usually means spacing sessions out, reviewing what you learned and what your early warning signs are, and writing down what to do if things slip. I also like to offer booster sessions: a check-in a month or two after ending, and an open door if a big transition comes up later.

If progress stalls, that is a conversation too. Sometimes it means a different approach, sometimes a referral, and sometimes it means the goal has shifted and we need to say so.

Common questions

How often should I go to therapy?

Weekly is the standard starting point, and the research on routine care suggests weekly sessions speed up improvement compared with less frequent schedules. Once you are making steady progress, every other week often works well. Monthly tends to suit maintenance after the main work is done rather than the early phase, when momentum matters most.

When does therapy start working?

Many people notice some relief within the first few sessions, often from being heard and having a plan. Measurable change in symptoms usually shows up over the first six to eight weeks. Deeper shifts in long-standing patterns take longer. If you feel nothing is moving after about eight sessions, say so, because that is the point to adjust the approach.

Can I stop therapy and come back later?

Yes. Planned endings with an open door are a normal part of good care. Some clients finish a focused course, take a break, and return months or years later for a new chapter such as a loss, a job change or a relationship shift. Booster sessions after ending are also common and can be scheduled whenever you need a check-in.
