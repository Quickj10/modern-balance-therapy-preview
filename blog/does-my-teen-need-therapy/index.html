Does My Teen Need Therapy? A Parent's Guide to the Signs

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC November 18, 2026 8 min read

The short answerMost teens have moody weeks. Therapy is worth considering when a change in mood, behavior, sleep, school or friendships lasts two weeks or more, is more intense than usual for your teen, and starts to interfere with daily life. If there is any talk of self-harm or suicide, act today: call or text 988.

Key takeaways

  • Judge by duration, intensity, change from your teen's baseline and interference with daily life, not by any single behavior.
  • In 2023, 40% of U.S. high school students reported persistent sadness or hopelessness, and only about four in ten teens with major depression get treatment.
  • CBT and Trauma-Focused CBT have strong evidence for teen anxiety, depression and trauma, including when delivered with parents involved.
  • Invite rather than force, offer a choice of therapist, and expect the therapist to keep session details private except when safety is at risk.
  • Any talk of suicide, new self-harm, or refusing food means call or text 988 today instead of waiting for an appointment.

Is this normal teenage behavior or a warning sign?

Adolescence is loud. Doors close harder, moods swing, and the kid who used to tell you everything now answers in one syllable. A lot of that is normal development, and it does not mean something is wrong.

In my practice, I ask parents to look at four things instead of any single behavior: duration, intensity, change from baseline, and interference.

  • Duration. A rough week is a week. A low or anxious stretch that shows up most days for two weeks or more is a pattern.
  • Intensity. Irritable is different from rageful. Nervous before a test is different from panic that keeps them home.
  • Change from baseline. You know your teen. If your talkative kid has gone quiet, or your easygoing kid is suddenly on edge, that shift matters more than how they compare with other teens.
  • Interference. Are school, friendships, sleep or family life getting harder because of it? Interference is the clearest sign that support would help.

If two or more of those are true, you are not overreacting by looking into therapy.

What are the signs a teenager might need therapy?

No single item on this list means your teen has a diagnosis. Think of it as a map of where to look.

Mood

  • Sadness, emptiness or irritability most days for two weeks or longer
  • Worry that is hard to switch off, or frequent panic
  • Big swings between very high and very low energy
  • Comments like "nothing matters" or "everyone would be better off without me"

Behavior

  • Pulling away from friends and activities they used to enjoy
  • New secrecy, lying, or explosive anger that is out of character
  • Self-harm, or covering arms and legs in warm weather
  • Risk-taking that scares you

School

  • Grades dropping without a clear reason
  • Refusing to go, frequent nurse visits, or stomachaches on school mornings
  • Teachers reaching out about focus, tears or conflict

Body

  • Sleeping much more or much less, or being awake most of the night
  • Appetite or weight changes, skipping meals, or new rules about food
  • Headaches and stomachaches with no medical cause

Online life

  • Phone use that replaces sleep, meals and in-person time
  • Visible distress after being online, or being targeted by peers
  • Feeds that center on self-harm, extreme dieting or hopelessness

Substances

  • Alcohol, vaping or cannabis, especially used to sleep, calm down or feel numb
  • Paraphernalia in their room, or the smell on their clothes

After a frightening or painful event, such as an accident, a loss, abuse or bullying, watch especially for nightmares, jumpiness, avoiding reminders, and a sense that the world is no longer safe. Trauma is my specialty, and these reactions are very treatable.

How common are anxiety and depression in teens?

More common than most parents expect, which is one reason I want you to feel less alone with this.

The CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey found that 40% of U.S. high school students experienced persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness in 2023, up from 30% in 2013. Among girls the figure was 53%. One in five students seriously considered attempting suicide, and 9% made an attempt.1

The National Institute of Mental Health estimates that 20.1% of adolescents ages 12 to 17, about 5 million teens, had a major depressive episode in 2021, and only 40.6% of them received treatment.2 An estimated 31.9% of adolescents ages 13 to 18 will have an anxiety disorder at some point.3

I share these numbers for one reason: the problem is common and the help gap is wide. If your teen is struggling, they are far from alone, and getting support early is what changes the trajectory.

How do I talk to my teen about therapy?

Lead with what you have noticed, not with a label. Keep it short, and expect a shrug the first time.

  • "I've noticed you've seemed really worn down the last few weeks. I'm not mad, I'm worried. Can we talk about it?"
  • "You don't have to figure this out by yourself. Talking to someone outside the family is one option, and I'd like us to try it."
  • "If it's not a fit after a few sessions, we can find someone else. I just want you to have someone in your corner."

A few things I have learned from years of working with families:

  • Do not force it. A teen dragged into therapy often spends the first month proving it will not work. Invite, revisit, and keep the door open.
  • Offer a choice. Let them pick between two or three therapists, or at least sit in on the consult call. Choice turns "my parents are making me" into "I chose this."
  • Normalize it. If you or someone they respect has been to therapy, say so.
  • Keep it separate from punishment. Therapy is not a consequence for bad grades or a bad attitude. If it starts that way, it rarely goes well.

Will the therapist tell me what my teen says?

This is the question every parent asks, and it is the question every teen is secretly asking too. Here is the plain-language version.

For therapy to work, your teen needs a space where they can be honest. So in general, the details of what a teen says in session stay between the teen and the therapist. What parents typically hear about is the big picture: how your teen is doing overall, what we are working on, and how you can help at home.

The exception is safety. If I learn that a teen is in danger, whether from themselves or from someone else, parents are brought in. Teens are told this at the very start, so there are no surprises.

The specific rules about what minors can consent to and what parents can access vary by state. I walk every family through how this works in Texas at the first consult, and we agree together on what gets shared and how, before therapy starts.

What does teen therapy look like over video?

Most of the teens I see are relaxed about video. It is where they already live, and talking from their own room often feels safer than an unfamiliar office.

A typical session is 50 minutes over a secure, private video link. The first one or two are about getting to know each other and setting goals in your teen's own words.

After that, sessions are a mix of talking, skills practice, and sometimes drawing, worksheets or games on screen. Modern Balance Therapy sees clients ages 12 and up by secure video anywhere in Texas, so a move across town or a semester away does not interrupt care.

What we need from home: a door that closes, headphones, and an agreement that the session is private. I ask parents to be nearby but not in the room unless we have planned a family portion.

Does therapy actually work for teens?

Yes, and the evidence is substantial. A meta-analysis of 447 randomized trials involving 30,431 young people found an average post-treatment effect size of 0.46, a medium effect, with the strongest results for anxiety (0.61).4 A Cochrane review of 87 studies found that 49.4% of children and teens were in remission from their main anxiety diagnosis after cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), compared with 17.8% of those on a waitlist.7

For trauma, Trauma-Focused CBT (TF-CBT) is the best-studied approach. In a two-site randomized trial of 229 children ages 8 to 14 with sexual abuse related PTSD symptoms, those who received TF-CBT improved significantly more than those in supportive child-centered therapy on PTSD, depression, behavior problems and shame, and their parents improved too.5 A network meta-analysis of 32 trials and 2,260 young people confirmed that individual trauma-focused CBT produced consistently large reductions in PTSD symptoms, while supportive counseling alone did not appear effective.6

I use CBT and TF-CBT because they work, and I blend in family systems and person-centered approaches because teens need to feel understood before they will try a single skill.

How are parents included?

You are part of the treatment, not a bystander. In TF-CBT, caregivers have their own component: learning what trauma does to a developing brain, how to respond to big reactions, and how to support the hard work your teen is doing. In the trial above, that is part of why parents improved along with their kids.5

For other concerns I offer parent coaching: short, practical sessions on how to respond to anxiety without feeding it, how to set limits without escalating, and how to keep the relationship warm while the hard stuff gets sorted. Most families find a few of these go a long way.

When should I act today instead of waiting?

Most of what I have described can wait for a consult this week. Some things cannot.

  • Any talk of wanting to die, not wanting to be here, or giving belongings away
  • Self-harm that is new or getting worse
  • Not eating or drinking, or a sudden large weight loss
  • Seeing or hearing things others do not, or confusion that does not make sense
  • Threats to hurt someone else

If you see any of these, do not wait for an appointment. Reach out to 988 right away, or go to the nearest emergency room.

If it is less urgent but you are still unsure, that is exactly what a free consult is for. You can tell me what you have been noticing, and I will give you an honest read on whether therapy makes sense now. Book a free consult to talk it through.

If you are in crisis: call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, available 24/7, or call 911 if you or your teen are in immediate danger.

Common questions

What age can a teenager start therapy?

There is no minimum age for benefiting from support, but most practices set their own range. I see clients ages 12 and up, with sessions adapted to how the teen communicates. Younger teens often do more skills practice and family work, while older teens tend to want more talk-based sessions with clear goals they chose themselves.

Can my teen do therapy without me in the room?

Yes, and most of the time that is the goal. Teens open up more when the session is theirs. Parents are usually included at the start for history and goals, periodically for progress updates, and for parent coaching or family sessions when those would help. We agree on that rhythm together at the consult.

What if my teen refuses to go to therapy?

Refusal is common at first. Try naming what you have noticed without a label, offer a choice of therapist, and suggest one consult call with no commitment. If they still refuse, parent coaching on its own can change the home dynamic and often opens the door later. Safety concerns are the exception, where waiting is not an option.
