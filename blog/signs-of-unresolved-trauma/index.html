Signs of Unresolved Trauma, and How Trauma Therapy Actually Helps

By Jackie McGreggor, MA, LPC July 14, 2026 8 min read

The short answerUnresolved trauma shows up sideways: a jumpy or exhausted body, fast anger or sudden fear, trouble trusting people, avoiding certain places or memories, and a quiet belief that you are damaged. Most people who experience trauma do not develop PTSD, but trauma can still shape daily life, and therapies like TF-CBT, CPT, PE and EMDR have strong evidence behind them.

Key takeaways

  • About 70 percent of people experience at least one traumatic event, but only about 6.8 percent of U.S. adults develop PTSD in their lifetime, so trauma and PTSD are not the same thing.
  • Signs of unresolved trauma cluster in five areas: the body, mood, relationships, avoidance, and how you see yourself.
  • Small t trauma (repeated, non-life-threatening hurts) and complex trauma (prolonged or repeated trauma, often in childhood) can leave the same fingerprints as a single catastrophic event.
  • Trauma-focused CBT, CPT, PE and EMDR carry strong recommendations from the 2023 VA/DoD guideline and robust support from reviews of more than 100 randomized trials.
  • Trauma therapy starts with stabilization and skills, not with retelling everything on day one, and you set the pace.

What counts as trauma?

Trauma is not defined by how dramatic an event looks from the outside. It is defined by what the event did to you: it overwhelmed your ability to cope at the time, and some part of you is still bracing for it. A car accident, a violent relationship, a sudden death, a childhood where no one was safe to go to, a medical emergency, all of these can qualify.

Trauma is also far more common than most people assume. In the World Health Organization's World Mental Health Surveys, which interviewed 68,894 people across 24 countries, roughly 70 percent reported at least one traumatic event in their lifetime, with an average of 3.2 events per person.1 If you have been through something hard, you are in the majority, not the exception.

Does every trauma turn into PTSD?

No, and this distinction matters. Posttraumatic stress disorder is a specific diagnosis with a specific pattern: intrusive memories, avoidance, negative shifts in mood and thinking, and a nervous system stuck on high alert, lasting more than a month and interfering with daily life. Most people who experience trauma do not develop it.

The National Institute of Mental Health estimates that about 6.8 percent of U.S. adults meet criteria for PTSD at some point in their lives, and about 3.6 percent did in the past year. Women are affected at roughly three times the rate of men in a given year (5.2 percent versus 1.8 percent), and about 5 percent of adolescents have had PTSD.2

Compare that 6.8 percent with the 70 percent who experience trauma and you can see the gap. The WHO data also showed the risk is not evenly spread: interpersonal violence, especially sexual violence, carries the highest risk of PTSD, and earlier trauma raises the risk from later trauma.1

Here is the part I want you to hear. You do not need a PTSD diagnosis for trauma to be shaping your life, and you do not need one to benefit from trauma therapy. Many of the people I work with fall short of the full diagnosis and are still carrying something that costs them every day.

What are the signs of unresolved trauma?

Unresolved trauma rarely announces itself as trauma. It tends to show up sideways: in the body, in mood, in relationships, in the things you avoid, and in how you see yourself.

Below are patterns people commonly describe. Recognizing a few of them does not mean you have a disorder; it means it may be worth a conversation with someone trained to sort it out.

In your body

  • Startling easily, or feeling jumpy in ordinary situations
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep, nightmares, or waking up exhausted
  • Chronic tension, headaches, stomach problems or pain that doctors cannot fully explain
  • Feeling numb or checked out at times, as if you are watching your life from a distance

In your mood

  • Irritability or anger that arrives faster and bigger than the situation deserves
  • Anxiety that does not have an obvious cause
  • Flat, low or hopeless stretches that come and go
  • Sudden waves of fear, shame or grief set off by a smell, a sound or a date on the calendar

In your relationships

  • Difficulty trusting people, even people who have earned it
  • Pulling away when someone gets close, or holding on hard and then feeling ashamed of it
  • Repeating the same painful relationship pattern with different people
  • Feeling that no one really knows you

In what you avoid

  • Steering around certain places, people, conversations or anniversaries
  • Staying busy, scrolling, drinking or working so you never have to sit with quiet
  • Gaps in memory around certain periods of your life
  • Changing the subject, even inside your own head, when something gets close

In how you see yourself

  • A deep sense that you are damaged, bad or not enough, held as a fact rather than a feeling
  • Guilt about what you did or did not do during the event
  • Believing the world is fundamentally unsafe and other people cannot be relied on
  • Difficulty imagining a future that goes well

What do "small t" trauma and complex trauma mean?

You may have seen the phrases "big T" and "small t" trauma. Big T usually refers to the events most people recognize as traumatic: assault, combat, disasters, serious accidents. Small t refers to experiences that did not threaten your life but still overwhelmed you, especially when they repeated: chronic criticism, emotional neglect, bullying, a parent's addiction, a long and humiliating divorce.

The nervous system does not grade on drama. Small t experiences, stacked over years, can leave the same fingerprints as a single catastrophic event.

Complex trauma, and the related diagnosis of complex PTSD in the ICD-11, usually describes what follows prolonged or repeated trauma, often beginning in childhood. Alongside the core PTSD symptoms, the National Center for PTSD describes three added areas of difficulty: regulating emotions, a lasting sense of worthlessness or shame, and trouble with closeness in relationships.3 If the "self-view" and "relationships" lists above hit hardest, this may be the frame that fits.

How does trauma therapy actually help?

The good news is that trauma treatment is one of the best-studied areas in psychotherapy. A 2020 systematic review of 114 randomized trials with 8,171 participants found robust evidence that trauma-focused CBT and EMDR produce clinically important improvement, with Cognitive Processing Therapy, Cognitive Therapy and Prolonged Exposure carrying the strongest evidence among the CBT approaches.4

The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline reached the same place. It gives a strong recommendation to Cognitive Processing Therapy, EMDR and Prolonged Exposure, and recommends these therapies over medication when both are available.5 Here is what each approach is, in one line.

  • Trauma-Focused CBT (TF-CBT): a structured approach, developed for children and teens with a caregiver involved, that builds coping skills first and then helps you tell and reframe the trauma story at a pace you control. The evidence was rated high across ten randomized trials.6
  • Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT): a roughly 12-session approach that targets the "stuck points," the beliefs about blame, safety, trust and self-worth that keep the trauma alive.
  • Prolonged Exposure (PE): a method that helps you gradually approach the memories and situations you have been avoiding, so they lose their power to hijack you.
  • Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR): a therapy that pairs brief attention to the memory with side-to-side eye movements or taps, helping the brain file the memory as past rather than present. In a network meta-analysis of 90 trials and 6,560 people, EMDR and trauma-focused CBT showed the largest effects versus wait list, with standardized mean differences of -2.07 and -1.46.7

In my practice I use TF-CBT and draw on CBT, psychodynamic and family systems work depending on what you need. If I think a different approach would serve you better, I will say so and help you find it.

What are the first sessions of trauma therapy like?

This is where I want to correct the biggest fear people bring. You do not walk in and retell everything on day one. Good trauma therapy starts with stabilization: understanding what is happening in your nervous system, building skills to settle it, and establishing enough safety in the relationship that the harder work becomes possible.

In the first session or two, we talk about what brought you in, what you want to be different, and what you are already doing to cope. I will ask about the trauma in broad strokes only, and you decide how much detail is enough. The National Center for PTSD notes that the leading trauma-focused therapies typically run about 8 to 16 sessions, and the trauma-focused part, where we work directly with the memory and its meaning, comes once you have the footing for it.8

Along the way, you set the pace. If a session gets too intense, we slow down. The goal is not to relive the event; it is to reach a place where the memory is something that happened to you, rather than something that is still happening.

Common questions

Do I need to remember everything that happened for trauma therapy to work?

No. Memory gaps are common after trauma, and the goal of therapy is not to recover every detail. We work with what you do remember, with the beliefs and reactions the experience left behind, and with what is happening in your body now. Many people make full progress without ever reconstructing a complete timeline, and I will never push you to fill in blanks.

Can trauma therapy be done online?

Yes. Several randomized trials, including VA studies of Prolonged Exposure and Cognitive Processing Therapy, found video delivery as effective as in-person treatment for PTSD, and the current VA/DoD guideline recommends secure video for these therapies. Modern Balance Therapy is fully virtual. We build in grounding skills and a safety plan so that intense moments can be handled well from home.

How do I know whether it is trauma or just anxiety?

Often you cannot tell from the outside, because trauma frequently wears anxiety or depression as its outer layer. The clue is usually the history: anxiety that started after something happened, gets triggered by reminders, or sits alongside avoidance and a harsh view of yourself. A careful assessment in the first sessions sorts this out, and the treatment plan follows from what we find.
